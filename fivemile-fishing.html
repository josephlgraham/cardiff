Five Mile Creek is small water, and small water tells on itself. Three things decide whether anything in it is interested today.

  • 🌡️How warm the water is. It sets what is feeding and how far a fish will move to eat.
  • 🌊How much is moving. The gauge at Republic says whether the channel is pushing.
  • 🧭What the weather is doing. A falling barometer ahead of a front is the oldest sign in fishing.

Catfish, bass, and bream all live in the lower creek through Graysville, Cardiff, and Brookside, along with gar, creek chub, and half a dozen kinds of sunfish. Alabama tests the fish once a year, and that counts for more on this creek than it does on most.

Reading the water

The fishing desk

—

Water temperature runs the whole thing, and oxygen decides the rest

Fish are cold blooded, so the temperature of the water sets how fast they digest, how much they have to eat, and how far they will move to get it. That is why the same creek feels empty in February and busy in June. What most fishing pages leave out is the other half: warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water does, and a fish short of oxygen stops feeding long before it is in any danger. It is why the worst fishing of the year here is not the coldest week but the hottest, stillest one in August.

Both numbers on this page are measured rather than guessed. The gauge at Republic reports the water temperature and the dissolved oxygen along with the stage, which is a good deal more than most creeks this size get.

Today's periods

Solunar
—

What this is

—

The moon passes overhead once a day and directly underfoot once a day, and it rises and sets in between. Anglers have fished those four times since John Alden Knight put the tables together in 1926: the two overhead passes are the major periods and run about two hours, and moonrise and moonset are the minor ones and run about one.

What it is, and what it is not

The times are astronomy and they are exact. Whether the fish agree is a prediction, not a measurement. Nothing on this creek reports a feeding period and this page is not pretending otherwise. What is worth knowing is that the two strongest periods of a month land near the new moon and the full moon, when the sun and the moon are pulling together, and that a period landing on top of first or last light counts for more than the same period at noon.

If you only get one trip, take the one where a major period and a sunrise or a sunset arrive within an hour of each other. That is the best this creek offers.

Four stages, and the fish do something different in each

Why a rise turns the fishing on before it turns it off

Rain running off the bank carries worms, grubs, and everything else that was living in the top inch of soil straight into the channel. For the first few hours of a rise the creek is carrying more food than it has all week, and the fish move to the edges to take it. That is the window.

What ends it is the current. Past a certain speed a fish spends more energy holding station than it gets back from feeding, so it drops out of the main channel and into whatever slack water it can find, and it stops eating until the water lets it start again. On the way back down the creek is still full of what the rain brought and no longer running hard, which is why the falling side of a rise is usually better than the rising side.

The fish in this creek

Thirteen, and counting

This list is not a survey. It is what readers of this site have caught or seen in the lower creek over the years, written down for the first time.

That makes it a first hand record rather than a complete one, and the difference matters. A state fisheries survey of Five Mile Creek below Republic would almost certainly turn up species nobody here has ever had on a hook, and it would settle the two entries below that are still stuck at the genus. If you fish this creek and catch something that is not here, send it in, and if you know of a survey, send that. Every one of them has a fuller entry in the field guide.

CatfishTwo of them, and they are not the same fish

Channel catfish

Ictalurus punctatus

Also called Channel cat, blue channel, fiddler

Works by smell and touch rather than sight, which is why it keeps feeding in colored water that shuts every other fish down.

Where
The deep bends and under cover through the day, out on the shallow edges to feed after dark.
Bait
Anything that smells. Cut bait, chicken liver, a nightcrawler fished on the bottom.
When
After dark, and the first few hours of a rise while the water is coloring up.

Blue catfish

Ictalurus furcatus

Also called Blue cat, blues

Bigger than the channel cat and it holds differently: out in the moving water rather than tucked under a bank.

Where
The main channel, in the deepest water you can reach with current going over it.
Bait
Fresh cut bait on the bottom, and bigger than feels reasonable.
When
Through the winter as well as the summer, which is what separates it from the channel cat.

BassThree of them here, and the creek suits the small ones

Largemouth bass

Micropterus salmoides

Also called Largemouth, black bass, trout

The top predator in the creek, and the fish everybody is really after. It does not patrol open water, it waits against something.

Where
Where a bend, a deep hole, and a fallen tree meet. In the slack water beside cover, not out in the current.
Bait
A live crayfish or a creek minnow. A soft plastic worked slowly along the bottom.
When
The pre-spawn weeks in March, and the fall feed in October. First and last light through the summer.

Spotted bass

Micropterus punctulatus

Also called Spot, Kentucky bass

The bass that suits a creek. It takes current better than a largemouth and turns up in water too small and too fast for one.

Where
The seam where fast water meets slow, over gravel bars and rock ledges. Usually within a rod length of the bank.
Bait
Smaller than you would throw at a largemouth. A live crayfish, or something small worked on the bottom.
When
All day through spring and fall. First light in July and August.

Rock bass

Ambloplites sp.

Also called Goggle eye, red eye

Short, deep, red eyed, and it fights above its weight. Which rock bass this creek holds has never been settled.

Where
Rocky runs and root wads in moving water, tight to the stone rather than out in the pool.
Bait
A small live bait or a tiny jig, worked slowly along the bottom.
When
Overcast days, and the low light at either end of one.

BreamFive kinds of sunfish, and most people call all of them bream

Bluegill

Lepomis macrochirus

Also called Bream, brim, sun perch

The fish nearly everybody here caught first. It beds in colonies from late spring right through summer, so a few yards of bank can hold dozens at once.

Where
Shallow bank, shade, and anything hanging over the water. On the beds all summer.
Bait
A cricket under a bobber on a cane pole, and nothing has improved on it. Red worms work.
When
Late spring through summer, and all day long while they are bedding.

Redear sunfish

Lepomis microlophus

Also called Shellcracker, red ear

Eats snails and crushes the shells, which is a food almost nothing else in the creek is competing for. Heavier than a bluegill and far less willing to come up for a bait.

Where
On the bottom over hard ground, and deeper than you would fish for bluegill.
Bait
Red worm on the bottom with no float, in about four feet of water.
When
Spring, and earlier in it than the bluegill. The shellcracker run is usually over before the bream run starts.

Longear sunfish

Lepomis megalotis

Also called Longear, red belly

The most colored fish in the creek and one of the smallest. Orange and blue with a long dark ear flap, and rarely bigger than a hand.

Where
Shallow gravel with a little current running over it. More current than a bluegill wants.
Bait
Anything small. Most of them are caught by somebody fishing for something else.
When
Summer. Look at it properly before you unhook it, because it is worth looking at.

In the field guide →

Green sunfish

Lepomis cyanellus

Also called Green perch, black perch

The toughest sunfish here. Bigger mouthed than a bluegill and stubborn about water that has gone too warm, too still, and too low for anything else.

Where
Cut banks, small feeder branches, and the pools that have shrunk to almost nothing by August.
Bait
Small hook tight to the bank. It will also take a lure meant for a bass and sometimes hold on to it.
When
The hot low weeks when nothing else is cooperating. That is the whole point of this fish.

In the field guide →

Pumpkinseed

Lepomis gibbosus

Also called Punkinseed, sunny

Small, round, and covered in orange spotting, with a red dot at the back of the ear flap that a bluegill does not have.

Where
Quiet weedy edges rather than current. The opposite water from a longear.
Bait
Worm under a float, kept moving along the edge rather than left sitting.
When
Spring and early summer. Scarce enough here that one coming up is worth a second look.

The rest of itThree you will meet whether you were fishing for them or not

Gar

Lepisosteus sp.

Also called Garfish, needle nose

Long, armored, and unmistakable. It hangs just under the surface of slow water and holds so still that it reads as a sunken branch until it moves.

Where
The long flat pools, near the top of the water, in the low light at either end of a hot day.
Bait
This is a fish to watch rather than a fish to catch. The mouth is bone and needle teeth and it does not take a hook the way anything else does.
When
High summer. It gulps air at the surface, so it is still there in warm low water that has driven everything else deep.

In the field guide →

Creek chub

Semotilus atromaculatus

Also called Chub, horny head

The fish most people here caught first without ever learning its name. Stout, striped, and willing to take almost anything.

Where
Riffles and the heads of pools, in the smallest water that holds fish at all.
Bait
A bare hook and a scrap of worm. Nothing else is needed and nothing else is better.
When
All year. A creek that holds chub is a creek that holds food, which is most of what a bass is looking for.

In the field guide →

Shiners

Cyprinella and Notropis

Also called Minnows, shiner minnows

The silver minnows that flash in a shallow run when the light catches them. Several species, not easily separated in a bucket, and the middle of the whole food web here.

Where
Open shallow runs in the light. Stand still on a bright day and watch the bottom.
Bait
Not a target. What may be caught and used as bait is set by the state and it changes, so read the current rules first.
When
All year, and at their best in spring, when the males of some of them turn blue, gold, or orange.

Before you eat what you catch

The state checks the fish. Check it before you eat any.

Once a year the state tests fish caught around Alabama. It says which ones are safe to eat, and how much of them. It goes creek by creek and fish by fish, from samples taken the fall before.

This creek carried industry for the better part of a hundred years. That check counts for more here than it does in most places.

The EPA grades creeks on what they are fit for. One of those grades is whether the fish are safe to eat. Five Mile Creek does not pass that one right now.

So read the advisory before you keep anything out of this water. What is allowed to go into the creek is on what goes into the creek. So is what the water has been measuring.
