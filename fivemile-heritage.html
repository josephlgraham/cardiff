Heritage

Archive →

Graysville, Cardiff, and Brookside sit in a row along the last stretch of Five Mile Creek, and two of them are here because somebody found coal under the farmland and could not hire enough men to dig it. Cardiff's owners wrote home to Wales and Scotland. Sloss sent its recruiters for Brookside to the far side of the Austro Hungarian Empire. Graysville had a cotton gin and never had a company at all. What came of it was a Russian Orthodox parish in Alabama, families from four countries living in a town of a few hundred people, and three places that have been arguing with the water ever since.

The coal did not stop where the charters did. Sloss worked the mine at Coalburg with prisoners leased from Jefferson County, and the slope at Republic was for a while the largest coal producer in the county. Thirteen more places sit along this creek besides the three that filed papers, and in the 1890 census the two biggest of the lot were Coalburg and Blossburg. Republic is also where the creek is measured, so every reading of the water on this site comes from there.

Seven chapters

1830s to now

What follows is the whole run in order, from the first farms on the bottomland to the greenway that goes over the old rail bed. Each chapter takes a stretch of years and sets the three towns inside it together, because they happened to each other. The mine at Cardiff opened six years before the one at Brookside. Graysville lost its charter seven years after the Brookside mine shut.

One company runs through most of it. Sloss ran the prison mine at Coalburg, bought the Brookside mine a year after it opened, and counted Cardiff among its camps as well.

Each chapter opens onto its own page, where the full dated record for those years is kept, every entry carrying the source it came from and a link to go and read it. The record is not evenly thick and the count under each chapter says how thick: Brookside was a Sloss town and got written about, Graysville was neither, and some decades of it are close to blank.

Before the coal

to 187901

It is easy to assume the coal was always the point here. For about fifty years it was not. Two of the three towns already had people living in them, and neither of them had any reason yet to be a town.

The camps

1880 to 188902

Cardiff went first. Two men bought the land in 1880, opened a mine, named it for home, and then filled the town by writing letters to Wales. Six years later Brookside did the same thing on a larger scale, and Sloss money was behind it inside a year.

It ended inside a year. The mine that built Brookside closed in 1920 and never reopened, the ovens came down, and seven years later the state took Graysville's charter away.

After the coal

Seventy years in one chapter, because the three spent it going three different ways. Cardiff emptied out, Graysville came back from the dead and grew, and Brookside held on and reached its own high water mark in 1980.

The creek that ran past the mines is the thing being cleaned up and walked along now, and the rail bed that hauled the coal out is the trail. None of which has stopped the creek flooding, and in 2003 it put water through downtown Brookside.

The three towns

West to east

The same three places side by side, which is the comparison the chapters keep making and this is the short version of. They were built for different reasons and they went different ways, and that is the part that gets flattened when people say the coal towns. Graysville had a gin, never belonged to a company, and is the largest of the three today. Cardiff was one mine and is now fifty two people. Brookside belonged to Sloss inside a year of its mine opening, and the parish its miners founded still puts on a food festival every November.

Along the creek

Sixteen places sit along this creek between Coalburg and the Graysville end, and three of them filed incorporation paperwork. That is the whole of the difference, and it is smaller than it sounds. In the 1890 census Coalburg had 842 people and Blossburg had 692, while Brookside and Cardiff together had 583. The Warner slope at Republic was for a while the largest coal producer in Jefferson County. None of those three ever took a charter.

What follows runs downstream, Coalburg first and the Graysville end last, and each place gets as much as the record carries. For several of them that is a post office and a date. For three it is a name on a map and nothing else, which is said here rather than left out, because somebody reading this may know the rest of it.

  1. 1879 to 1932

    Coalburg

    Coalburg is the oldest of these and the largest any of them got. John T. Milner opened the mines in 1879 and sold them in May 1883 to the Georgia Pacific Railway. Sloss took over the mines of the Coalburg Coal and Coke Company and worked north along the railroad from here, and by 1893 it had opened or bought mines at Brookside, Cardiff, and Blossburg. The company that opened the Brookside mine in 1886 was called Coalburg Coal and Coke.

    Sloss ran the Coalburg mine with prisoners leased from Jefferson County. Men were arrested on small charges, sold to the company, and sent underground. In 1895 the county health officer, Dr. Thomas D. Parke, reported a death rate at Coalburg of 90 men for every 1,000 prisoners. Between 1899 and 1902, more than 100 leased men died there. Nearly all of the county's prisoners were Black. Most of their names are gone. An explosion killed eleven miners here in 1890.

    There were 214 coke ovens at Coalburg by 1900, and in 1889 Sloss had put in a bank of 63 mechanically charged ovens of a patented design that the Library of Congress survey reckons may have been the first of its kind in the country. The census counted 842 people here in 1890, more than Brookside and Cardiff had between them. The post office ran from 1883 to 1932.

    The Equal Justice Initiative has written about convict leasing in Jefferson County, and Coalburg is in it.

    Wikipedia, Encyclopedia of Alabama, Encyclopedia of Alabama, Library of Congress, University of Alabama Libraries, Freshwater Land Trust, Equal Justice Initiative
  2. 1898 to now

    Republic

    Republic was called Warner first. The Pioneer Mining and Manufacturing Company opened its first mines in the back half of 1898, and the two it started with were Sayreton and the Warner slope here. By 1900 Warner was the largest coal producer in Jefferson County. In 1901 it sent out 344,357 tons on its own, which was more coal than every mine in Alabama put together had raised in 1880.

    Republic Iron and Steel bought the company in 1899 and the place took the new owner's name. The post office opened as Republic in 1903 and ran to 1968. There is a photograph of a slope mine here from 1908, captioned for the Warner Coal Company.

    The creek level on this site comes from Republic. The gauge there is the only one left reporting live water anywhere in the watershed, so every time you check the creek here, you are looking at Republic.

    Library of Congress, University of Alabama Libraries, Encyclopedia of Alabama, USGS 02457595
  3. —

    New Town

    New Town sits a few hundred yards off the water between Coalburg and Brookside. The US Geological Survey's names file carries it and OpenStreetMap carries it, and that is all that has turned up. It is not in the University of Alabama's list of Jefferson County places, there is no post office on record for it, and no date has come to hand for when anybody started calling it that.

    USGS Geographic Names Information System
  4. —

    Dogtown

    Dogtown is in the University of Alabama's list of Jefferson County places with a map reference beside it and no remark, which is that list's way of saying the name is real and the compiler had nothing to add. The names file puts it on the bank between Republic and Mineral Springs. No post office, no founding date, and no account of who was there has turned up.

    University of Alabama Libraries, USGS Geographic Names Information System
  5. 1908

    Mineral Springs

    Mineral Springs first appears on a map dating from 1908. That is the whole of the record in the sources this page uses. There is no post office on file for it and nothing that says what was at the spring.

    University of Alabama Libraries
  6. 1903

    Watson

    Watson had a post office from 1903. These sources do not say when it closed, who Watson was, or what stood there besides the post office.

    University of Alabama Libraries
  7. —

    Scarlet Town

    Scarlet Town is carried by the US Geological Survey's names file and by OpenStreetMap, a few hundred yards from the creek above Brookside. It is not in the University of Alabama's list, and no post office, date, or account of it has turned up anywhere else.

    USGS Geographic Names Information System
  8. 1983

    Daisey City

    Daisey City first appears on the 1983 edition of the county highway map, spelled with an e, which is also how the names file spells it. Nothing older than that has turned up in the sources this page uses.

    University of Alabama Libraries, USGS Geographic Names Information System
  9. 1886 to now

    Brookside

    Brookside is named for the creek that runs through it. The Coalburg Coal and Coke Company opened the mine in 1886 and Sloss bought it the year after, the post office opened in 1888, and the town took its charter in 1898. The census counted 380 people here in 1890 and 1,253 in 2020.

    Encyclopedia of Alabama, Wikipedia, University of Alabama Libraries
  10. 1910 to 1930

    Pinkney City

    Pinkney City is the odd one here, because it has census figures and almost nothing else. The census counted 358 people in 1910 and 177 in 1930. The University of Alabama's list has the name first appearing on a map dating from 1922, which is later than both of those counts, and nothing found so far says who Pinkney was or what the place was built around.

    University of Alabama Libraries
  11. 1880 to now

    Cardiff

    Cardiff is named for the Welsh city, by the men who opened the mine in 1880 and then wrote home to fill the town. The post office opened in 1890 and the charter came in January 1900, the year 562 people were counted here. In 2020 it was 52.

    Encyclopedia of Alabama, Wikipedia, University of Alabama Libraries
  12. 1889 to 1968

    Blossburg

    Blossburg is named for Blossburg in Pennsylvania, which was a coal town first. It was one of the mines Sloss had opened or bought by 1893, and it ended up the largest of them for coke: 350 ovens by 1900, against 214 at Coalburg and 99 at Brookside. The census counted 692 people here in 1890, more than Brookside and Cardiff had between them. The post office ran from 1889 to 1968, close to eighty years for a place that never filed a charter.

    University of Alabama Libraries, Library of Congress
  13. 1897 to now

    Graysville

    Graysville was Gintown before it was Graysville, after the only cotton gin for miles around. It is the one of the three that never belonged to a company, and the only one that has had to incorporate twice: chartered in 1897, stripped of the charter in 1927 for inactivity, and chartered again on November 17, 1945, mostly so it could build itself a water system. The census counted 319 people here in 1900 and 1,950 in 2020.

    Encyclopedia of Alabama, Wikipedia, University of Alabama Libraries
  14. 1928 to 1981

    Alden

    Alden was built as a mining camp in 1928 by Sloss-Sheffield Steel and Iron, for the men working its Flat Top mine. That makes it the last place on this creek put up around a mine, and it went up nearly fifty years after Coalburg. The company built 160 houses and a concrete man way 981 feet long running down to the mine entrance. The post office ran from 1934 to 1981.

    The name came out of a conversation between the superintendent, Howard Thomas, and the chairman, Hugh Morrow, who had offered fifty dollars for a good one. Thomas suggested his daughter's. Ruth Alden was ten years old, and she was guest of honor at the opening gala.

    Bhamwiki, University of Alabama Libraries
  15. 1887 to 1906

    Linn Crossing

    Linn Crossing has gone by four names. It was Chetora and it was Ono. Its own post office ran from 1887 to 1895, a Linn post office took over in 1895, and an Ono post office ran from 1895 to 1906. Nobody has turned up who Linn was.

    The creek has been sampled at Linn Crossing since June 1964, and that station's record runs to 2006. Those readings are in the creek room with everything else taken off this water.

    University of Alabama Libraries, US Geological Survey
  16. 1937

    Chetopa

    Chetopa first appears on the 1937 edition of the county highway map, and it is a different name from Chetora, which was Linn Crossing just down the road. The names file carries a Chetopa Mine impoundment about a mile east of it, so there was a mine. Nothing found so far dates it or says who worked it.

    University of Alabama Libraries, USGS Geographic Names Information System
Where this comes from

This is assembled reading rather than original research, and the difference is worth being plain about. Everything here comes from material that is public and online: the Encyclopedia of Alabama, Bhamwiki, Wikipedia, the Library of Congress, the University of Alabama's list of Jefferson County places, the census, the Freshwater Land Trust, the Equal Justice Initiative, the US Geological Survey, and the marker standing on the roadside in Cardiff. Every entry carries the one it came from with a link, so none of it asks to be taken on trust.

That leaves a great deal out. When a chapter says something is not recorded, it means it has not turned up in any of those, not that nobody ever wrote it down. Courthouse deeds, church registers, company payrolls and the boxes in people's closets are mostly not on the internet, and a good deal of what actually happened in these towns is only in them. Where the sources disagree with each other, which they do on a couple of the population counts, this page follows the census.

That rule earned its keep on the incorporation dates. The University of Alabama's list has Graysville incorporating in 1946 and Brookside in 1896, where this page says 1897 and 1898. The census counted Graysville in 1900 and again in 1910, so 1946 cannot be the first charter and 1897 stands, and the 1946 in that list is the first sitting of the council, on January 16, 1946, seven weeks after the second charter on November 17, 1945. Brookside the census cannot settle, because it counted the place in 1890 and in 1900, on both sides of both dates. That one goes with the weight of the sources instead, and the Encyclopedia of Alabama and Wikipedia both say 1898.

So if you know one of these answers, there is a fair chance you know more about it than this page does. That is not a problem with the page so much as the shape of the thing, and it is why the address at the bottom is there.
